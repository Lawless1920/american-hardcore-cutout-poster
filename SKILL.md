# American Hardcore Cutout Poster

Create one finished raster poster from one supplied photo. The output is an original editorial interpretation, not a copy of a reference image, its lettering, brand, watermark, or exact composition.

## Production Contract

- Treat the supplied customer photo as an **edit target**, not a style-only reference. Keep the selected hero subject recognizable: identity, defining markings, object geometry, pose when visible, and important local colors. A color source must remain color in the black-framed photo panel and its hero cutout; never desaturate it merely because the surrounding screen-print background uses a limited palette.
- Keep the source image's orientation. Match its aspect ratio by default unless the user explicitly supplies another output ratio.
- Classify the source before designing: use the **monochrome route** only when the supplied photo is genuinely black-and-white or grayscale. A dark, muted, desaturated, or sepia color photograph is still a color source and uses the fluorescent route.
- Treat the black-framed photo panel as a protected source-photography layer. Inside its border, retain a faithful crop of the supplied photograph: the real environment, perspective, lighting, local colors, people, objects, and spatial relationships must remain recognizably the same. Mild print grain is allowed, but the outer screen-print background must never bleed into, replace, repaint, or invent the framed scene. A foreground cutout may overlap the panel, but it is a separate overlay extracted from that same source and never licenses changing the panel's environmental content.
- Produce exactly one final poster. Do not create previews, options, or a batch unless asked.
- Invent a short, image-led English phrase of 2-4 words unless the user provides exact title text. It may be emotional or poetic, but must not use a visible brand, location, watermark, or copied reference wording. Treat a short connector or modifier as part of the same title—not as unrelated decorative text.
- Generate the finished raster image with the built-in image-generation capability. Inspect it before delivery. Regenerate at most once, and only to correct a wrong hero subject (including a collective subject reduced to one member), misspelled/missing title, unclear cutout, or a background that is not visibly derived from the source photo.

## First: Inspect And Choose The Hero

Read [the subject-selection guide](references/subject-selection.md) before choosing the hero.

1. Inspect the actual photo. Identify candidate people, animals, vehicles, objects, and scene landmarks; do not infer details that are not visible.
2. Select one primary visual event. Usually this is one person, animal, vehicle, or object. Apply the guide's collective-event and paired-foreground overrides before choosing an individual: when a team, band, crew, or other group is visibly united by one activity, formation, or shared moment—or when two foreground people jointly create the photograph's visual thesis—the complete subject set is the hero, not its most centered, sharpest, or most front-facing member.
3. Derive a compact emotional theme from the chosen subject and its visible context. Use that theme to invent the title and choose supporting doodles.
4. Preserve the hero subject at high fidelity. The background and graphic layers may change; the hero may not become an unrelated person, vehicle, animal, or object.

## Fixed Visual System

### Background

- Build a full-canvas graphic background from an enlarged, recognizable crop of the source: a facial detail, wheel, bark, fabric, architecture, water, foliage, terrain, body contour, or another distinctive texture/shape actually present in the image.
- Reinterpret this crop as rough two- or three-color screen printing: coarse halftone, ink scuffs, photocopy grain, blocky silhouette zones, and imperfect registration.
- For a color source, choose the background palette from the **hero's visible, distinctive local colors** first: extract one or two dominant or signature hues from the selected subject, then pair them with a source-derived support hue or black for contrast. Push those selected colors into hard-edged screen-print ink, but do not impose an unrelated fixed palette. For example, a yellow vehicle may lead to yellow + sky blue + black; a red guitar may lead to red + orange + black; a blue garment may lead to blue + violet + black.
- Use the former fixed pairs—**electric blue + black**, **hot orange + red**, or **acid green + black**—only as fallbacks when the subject has no clear or useful color signature. An off-white paper/ink ground is allowed as a support color inside the composition, never as a weathered outer margin.
- The source-derived background may retain a large recognizable echo of the hero. It must never be a generic unrelated gradient, stock texture, or invented cityscape.
- Render the screen-print field **full bleed to every canvas edge**. Coarse halftone, ink scuffs, and registration defects may live within the field, but never create an aged-paper perimeter, off-white deckled edge, torn border, faded vignette, or irregular blank margin around the poster.

### Color Source Route

When the supplied photo is color, keep the black-framed photo panel and the hero cutout in their recognizable original local colors, including subdued, dark, low-saturation, or sepia color. The surrounding source-derived field alone becomes the selected subject-color-adaptive high-energy screen-print palette. Do not convert the main photo or hero to monochrome.

### Monochrome Source Route

When the supplied photo is genuinely black-and-white or grayscale, replace the fluorescent-palette rule above for the photo treatment and source-derived background:

- Keep the central photo, enlarged background fragment, and screen-print field in black, charcoal, graphite gray, warm gray, and off-white only. Use two or three ink values with strong tonal contrast.
- Use chunky halftone, xerox-black ink, restrained cream-paper grain within the field, scuffs, and imperfect screen-print registration to make the monochrome field feel hard-edged rather than sterile; keep the outer canvas edge full bleed and clean.
- Do not introduce automatic electric blue, orange-red, acid green, or other fluorescent background ink merely to make a black-and-white source feel louder.
- White doodles remain white. The English ransom-letter title is the **only mandatory color exception**: it must remain a vivid multicolor collage even on a grayscale source, using at least four clearly different high-chroma color families across its primary letters. Do not render a grayscale-source title as black, gray, white, or mostly neutral, and do not offer a monochrome-title route.

### Main Image And Cutout

- Use the selected **Type 1** layout: a large central black-framed photo panel, usually about 70%-78% of canvas width and 64%-76% of canvas height, surrounded by an active graphic background.
- Set the panel at a subtle clockwise tilt of about 2°-4°. It must feel deliberately pasted/printed, never perfectly upright or dramatically diagonal. Preserve the source orientation inside the panel; adapt its scale rather than forcing a portrait crop.
- Construct the composition as two layers: a **mandatory black-framed, faithful source-photo panel** behind, plus a foreground line-cut hero from the same source. The frame remains a recognizable regular near-square or rectangle with at least three visible corners and a clearly readable perimeter; do not use a shaped, scalloped, notched, silhouette-following, or otherwise irregular photo border, and do not let the cutout or its shadow replace the frame itself.
- Keep the black frame deliberately slim and visually even, comparable to a strong printed keyline rather than a heavy black block: on exposed sides, target about **1.5%-2.25% of the panel's short side** with matching perceived weight on all four sides. A cutout may cover a short stretch when crossing the frame, but the remaining visible runs must not thicken, taper, or become a second shadow-border.
- Make controlled cross-frame overlap the default rather than an exception. The foreground cutout should deliberately cross one or two non-corner stretches of the frame—often at the head/hat, hand/instrument, shoulder, or feet—to create the pasted editorial depth seen in a concert or street poster. Keep most of the hero anchored inside the photographic panel; never make it an unmoored, frame-replacing silhouette. Cross-frame overlap must follow an actual visible source silhouette, not an invented enlargement.
- Inside the frame, show the original source environment as a coherent photograph behind the foreground cutout. Preserve the real setting, light, objects, and context required to understand the source; do not substitute the outer halftone field, use a generic graphic backdrop, or make the panel interior follow the outer background's altered palette or forms.
- The hero should be visually dominant at thumbnail scale. Do not use a soft oval, a neat scrapbook cutout, a generic sticker, or a polished product-ad frame.

### Collective Hero Treatment

When the selected visual event is a group or a paired foreground subject set, treat the complete set as one subject in the Type 1 composition.

- Draw one shared irregular line-cut contour around the collective silhouette; do not give one member a separate hero cutout, enlargement, or isolated outline.
- Crop and scale the framed source photo so the group occupies about 65%-75% of its active photographic area. Retain only the environmental cues needed to identify the shared event, rather than preserving a distant full scene that makes the group feel incidental.
- Keep the people, their relative positions, and relevant shared props recognizable. A ball, instrument, phone, or other held item may support the story, but must never decide which person becomes the hero.
- For two comparably important foreground people, draw one shared contour around both. Do not isolate just the sharper, more frontal, more central, or more fully visible person when the other person's silhouette, clothing, gaze, or counterpoint is necessary to the image's visual balance.

### Doodles

- Add 2-5 small, white, hand-drawn scene-aware marks in every poster: loose rings, bubbles, speed lines, a spray-can-like mark, drips, sparks, or an object-specific doodle.
- Use an object-specific doodle only when the source object is visually confirmed. Preserve the observable object category and geometry rather than inferring it from the setting: for example, distinguish a spherical association football from an oval rugby/American-football shape. When the object is small, blurred, occluded, or ambiguous, use neutral marks such as rings, arrows, speed lines, or texture instead of guessing a representational doodle.
- Place them around—not over—the hero's face or defining product detail. They should animate the layout, never become the main illustration or a cute cartoon layer.

### Ransom-Letter Title

- The English ransom-letter title is always a colorful collage layer. On a color source it adds a separate high-chroma accent to the color image; on a genuine grayscale source it is the only colored element—photo, frame, background, and doodles stay monochrome while the title remains vivid.
- Compose the title as a **distributed typographic collage**, not a compulsory single baseline or two adjacent rows. Arrange its words and individual letters around the cutout and photo panel: a vertical side stack, a staggered lower corner, separated clusters at two panel edges, or a cascade that responds to the image's empty areas. Keep the reading order clear even when the word shapes are spatially broken apart.
- Give the words an intentional hierarchy. Make the core emotional noun/verb the largest ransom-letter cluster. Use a smaller supporting word or a compact connective/modifier tag to create rhythm rather than making every word the same size.
- Scale the entire ransom-letter layer—large letters, mini word tags, and their paper backings—to **90% of the previously normal collage scale**. Keep the newly freed space as breathing room; do not compensate by spreading the title farther apart or increasing any title element. The hero and black-framed photo panel must gain the visual priority.
- A short word such as `the`, `new`, `cool`, `with`, `no`, or `now` may appear as one intact mini wordmark within a single narrow colored paper/print rectangle. Its letters must stay together, read as a continuous word, and be visibly smaller than the main title letters. Use at most two such tags and only when they belong to the generated phrase.
- Treat the title as a separate, exuberantly multicolored collage layer; it may deliberately break the poster background's two/three-color ink system. It must look assembled from heterogeneous found clippings—different magazines, newspapers, flyers, signage, and packaging—not like one digital typeface recolored onto matching cards. Build primary letters as individually cut paper/print characters with mixed upper/lower case, rough cut edges, rotations, halftone/ink texture, and a high-chroma color mix of red, orange, yellow, cobalt, cyan, turquoise, magenta, purple, acid green, black, and cream.
- Treat every primary ransom character as its own found-object design, not merely a different color tile. Each must visibly differ from its neighbors in at least **three** of these properties: type voice/case, cap height, width or aspect ratio, paper backing shape, rotation, outline treatment, print source/texture, and color. For a 5+ letter title, use at least five distinct character treatments across the full phrase; a compact one-piece connector tag is the only allowed exception.
- Use at least **three clearly different type voices** for a 2-5 letter title and at least **four** for a 6+ letter title: for example a heavy newspaper serif, a tall condensed sans, a slab serif, an italic display face, a stencil/sign letter, or a loose lower-case script. No two adjacent primary letters may share both the same type voice and the same backing-card geometry. Never typeset a complete main word in one consistent font, weight, baseline, or card shape.
- Make the main ransom letters strongly **uneven in scale**. Within a multi-letter main word, make the tallest full-size letter about **1.8-2.4×** the shortest, and include visibly tall/narrow, wide/short, and compact letter forms. Vary rotation and backing-scrap proportions while retaining a clear reading order. The key word cluster is largest, secondary-word letters are visibly smaller, and a continuous mini-word tag is smallest. Avoid a row, grid, or staircase of near-equal block capitals or same-size cards.
- Make every large character or its backing scrap visibly distinct from its neighbors in color as well as form. For a title with 5 or fewer large letters, use at least 4 distinct color families; for 6 or more, use at least 5. Do not let three adjacent large letters share the same dominant paper/ink color, and do not collapse the title into an all-red, all-orange, all-blue, or mostly neutral word block. Color variety is as important as scale, type treatment, and placement.
- Give every small connector/modifier tag a single high-contrast color block of its own—such as black on lemon yellow, white on cobalt, black on pink, or cream on red—so the full word remains intact and instantly legible without visually merging into the large ransom letters.
- Keep all words legible and spell the chosen phrase exactly. The visual language is vintage magazine/ransom-letter collage, not a reproduction of any supplied alphabet sheet or sample word.
- Do not add secondary paragraphs, fake logos, UI, social-media chrome, brands, dates, or watermarks.

## Prompt Compilation

Compile a concise, decisive prompt in this order:

1. **Output and source preservation:** source orientation/ratio, high-fidelity hero, exact title text, and a protected panel interior that retains the real source environment.
2. **Composition:** the large, slightly clockwise-tilted black-framed source-photo panel (about 70%-78% of canvas width), with a slim, even 1.5%-2.25%-of-short-side keyline and a regular rectangular/near-square perimeter, plus its foreground line-cut hero deliberately crossing one or two non-corner frame stretches while leaving the rectangle recognizable.
3. **Source-derived background:** name the exact visible source feature to enlarge and the selected color route. Apply this screen-print transformation only outside the protected photo panel. For a color source, explicitly preserve original colors and environment in the hero and black-framed photo panel while using a two/three-color subject-color-adaptive screen-print palette only for the surrounding field. For a genuinely grayscale source, use black/charcoal/gray/off-white screen-print values for the outer field only; reserve all vivid color for the title.
4. **Finishing:** white scene-aware doodles that either match a visually confirmed source object or remain abstract; an always-vivid, distributed ransom-letter hierarchy where every primary character has a distinct clipping identity, strongly unequal scale, varied card shapes, and mixed type voices; any high-contrast compact connector tag; coarse printing defects within a clean full-bleed background; and hard avoids.

State what must remain recognizable, what may change, and what must not appear. Use concrete visible nouns rather than abstract style labels.

## Quality Gate And Retry Limit

Inspect the finished image at full size and thumbnail size before returning it.

- The hero is the correct, recognizable source subject and dominates the image.
- When the hero is a collective event, the complete group remains one enlarged, recognizable unit within one shared line-cut contour. No member is singled out because of centrality or a held object, and the group occupies about 65%-75% of the framed photo's active area while the environment is only supporting context.
- When two foreground people jointly define the photograph, both remain inside one shared line-cut treatment. Face visibility, sharpness, centrality, contrast, or a more obvious prop can support a decision but cannot alone promote one person to the sole hero.
- The background visibly comes from a feature or enlarged fragment of the input photo.
- The background color/ink field reaches all four canvas edges without a worn-paper rim, torn edge, off-white border, or faded perimeter.
- The main photo is a faithful, recognizably original source scene inside a slightly clockwise-tilted black rectangular/near-square frame: its environment, perspective, lighting, objects, and local colors have not been replaced by the outer screen-print field. The border is a regular, slim, even keyline—about 1.5%-2.25% of the panel's short side on exposed runs—not an oversized black block, irregular shaped window, or extra shadow border. A foreground cutout may intentionally cross one or two non-corner frame stretches, but at least three corners and the rectangle's perimeter remain clearly readable; it stays visibly anchored to the panel and never replaces the frame.
- For a color source, the hero and black-framed photo panel retain their recognizable original colors; only the source-derived surrounding background uses a two- or three-color palette selected from the hero's distinctive visible colors, with a source-derived support hue or black when needed for contrast. Fixed electric-blue, orange-red, or acid-green presets appear only when the hero supplies no useful dominant color. For a genuinely grayscale source, photo treatment, black frame, background, and doodles use only black, charcoal, gray, and off-white values. The English title alone must remain visibly multicolor, with at least four distinct high-chroma color families; a black-and-white or mostly neutral title fails this gate.
- White doodles are present, sparse, and appropriate to the scene. Any object-specific doodle matches the visible source object's category and geometry; when the source evidence is ambiguous, the doodle stays abstract rather than inventing a different object or sports ball.
- The title contains 2-4 correctly spelled English words, is legible, and is distributed around the composition rather than forced into one/two adjacent baselines. Its complete collage layer is 90% of the prior normal title scale and leaves the hero visually dominant. Every primary character has a distinct collage identity, differing from its neighbors in at least three properties among type/case, cap height, aspect ratio, backing shape, rotation, outline, texture, and color. It uses at least three type voices for a short title and four for a 6+ letter title; within a main word, the tallest full-size primary character is about 1.8-2.4× the shortest, with tall/narrow, wide/short, and compact forms rather than a uniform font, grid, or same-size colored tiles. Primary title letters use at least 4-5 visibly distinct colors with no repetitive adjacent color run; any small connector/modifier tag is continuous inside its own high-contrast colored rectangle and supports the same phrase.
- No copied reference text/identity, generic UI, sleek brand campaign look, glossy 3D, photorealistic background replacement, neon-cyberpunk look, cute/cartoon decoration, or unrelated extra hero appears.

If and only if one listed production-contract failure occurs, regenerate once with the single failed constraint strengthened. If the second image still fails, report the limitation instead of silently generating more images.

## Return Format

Show the final image. State the selected hero and generated title in one short line. Include the final generation prompt only when the user asks for it.
